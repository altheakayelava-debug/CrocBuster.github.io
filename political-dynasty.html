According to the article The Ruling Family: How Political Dynasties Are Destroying Democracy in the Philippines, a large majority of elected officials come from political dynasties, meaning that elections often pass power among established families rather than offering real democratic choice. These dynasties limit electoral competition, favoring name recognition, wealth, and political machinery over merit or platforms. This concentration of power encourages corruption, patronage, and nepotism, as seen in high-profile cases like the 2013 pork barrel scandal, and makes it difficult to hold officials accountable. Even after scandals, members of dynastic families often return to office, using their influence and networks to maintain control. The article argues that this cycle undermines democracy, discourages new leaders from emerging, reinforces inequality, and erodes institutional checks and balances, turning elections into formalities that legitimize the rule of a few powerful clans.

The CN2024‑04 report defines a political dynasty as a family where at least two members win elected positions in at least two elections, often holding different offices at the same time or one after another. In the Philippines, these dynasties concentrate power in the hands of a few families, limiting fair competition, reducing chances for ordinary citizens to run for office, and often resulting in public resources benefiting only a small circle. Research cited in CN2024‑04 shows that dynasties are often linked to corruption, favoritism, weak accountability, and slower local development. Although the 1987 Constitution says dynasties should be prohibited “as may be defined by law,” Congress has never passed a law to regulate them, allowing political families to rotate members through positions or areas to maintain power. Breaking the cycle of dynastic rule through new laws, voter awareness, or civic engagement is essential for transparent and accountable governance.
